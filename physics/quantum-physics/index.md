# Quantum Physics

**Quantum physics** emerged from a crisis in classical physics.

At the end of the nineteenth century, several physical phenomena revealed limits in the classical description of matter and radiation. Among them, the problem of **blackbody radiation** played a decisive role: attempts to describe its spectrum ultimately led Max Planck to introduce the idea that energy exchange occurs in discrete quantities.

This section explores the physical problems, mathematical ideas, and conceptual transformations that contributed to the development of quantum theory.

<div class="content-box">

## Blackbody Radiation and the Quantum Revolution

Blackbody radiation stands at one of the turning points in the history of physics.

The attempt to understand the observed spectrum of thermal radiation brought together thermodynamics, electromagnetism, statistical reasoning, and experiment. The failure of classical predictions at high frequencies exposed a fundamental problem that became known as the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

Planck's solution introduced a radically new assumption: energy could be exchanged in discrete quantities proportional to frequency.

$$
E=h\nu
$$

What began as a problem concerning thermal radiation became one of the foundations of quantum physics.

## From Classical Physics to Quantum Theory

The birth of quantum physics was not simply the introduction of a new formula. It represented a profound change in the conceptual structure of physical theory.

Classical physics had achieved remarkable success in describing mechanics, thermodynamics, and electromagnetic phenomena. Yet the interaction between matter and radiation revealed regimes in which classical assumptions could no longer reproduce experimental observations.

The quantum hypothesis emerged from this tension between **theoretical structure and empirical evidence**.

The development of quantum theory therefore offers an especially clear example of how physics evolves: established models are tested against nature, their limits become visible, and new conceptual frameworks emerge when the old ones are no longer sufficient.

</div>

## Why Quantum Physics Matters

Quantum physics transformed our understanding of matter, radiation, measurement, and the relation between physical theory and observable phenomena.

Its historical development also illustrates a broader lesson about scientific knowledge: successful theories are not necessarily final theories. A mathematical framework may describe a vast domain of phenomena with extraordinary precision while still encountering boundaries beyond which new principles are required.

The quantum revolution is therefore both a chapter in physics and a fundamental case study in the evolution of scientific thought.
